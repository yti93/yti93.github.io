Pachinko

Min Jin Lee · ★★★★☆ · Nov 2026

A great sprawling arc of a multigenerational narrative. I think for so long I’ve been guilty of this Western view of Japan as this placid, uniform society with only vague knowledge of its 20th century transformation, which was only reinforced by popular (Western) media or Japanese media that has made it into American pop culture, only reading classic novels and authors about Japanese-ness. While there is a paradox in pointing to Pachinko, which has received critical acclaim in Western circles, as narrative altering, it indeed has shifted my thinking of Japanese and Korean-Japanese society in a fundamental way.

Not what I expected; the only thing keeping it from a five star rating from me was the ending in the late 80s felt a bit harried but overall a stellar book and worthy of its accolades. The family contained multitudes without feeling over-the-top in terms of caricature.

So Long, See You Tomorrow

William Maxwell · ★★★★☆ · Oct 2026

"What we, or at any rate, what I, refer to confidently as memory - meaning a moment, a scene, a fact that has been subjected to a fixative and thereby rescued from oblivion - is really a form of storytelling that goes on continually in the mind and often changes with the telling. Too many conflicting emotional interests are involved for life ever to be wholly acceptable, and possibly it is the work of the storyteller to rearrange things so that they conform to this end. In any case, in talking about the past we lie with every breath we draw."

This book is already short and quick, but if it were to be boiled down to one phrase, this would be it. A small town murder is relayed over a hundred-odd pages from the vantage point of not only the narrator but close friends and family. Depending on who's vantage point the reader inteprets this from, this murder is (in)explicable, (in)validated, and/or (in)congruous.

Underworld

Don DeLillo · ★★★☆☆ · Oct 2026

7 years after I was gifted this book and read the prologue, I picked it up. I'm glad I did.

Maybe I'm too dumb to get it, maybe I'm out of touch with the state of the world when this came out, but the book felt dated (which it is technically, being almost 30 years old), and it felt overlong. I did some reading about the book as I was reading it, and it felt overwrought. I had issues with the repetitive prose (felt mundane) and some of the subplots (Texas Highway Killer, Lenny Bruce, etc) felt like filler. I enjoyed the Shays (wish Matty got more play), and wish there was more time spent in the '50s and '90s versus the '70s and '80s.

As a reflection on post-WWII America, themes of waste, excess, existentialism, and religion all felt spot-on, but I felt as if Underworld was trying to do too much, and trying to get the reader to do too much interpretation under the veneer of personal salvation as seen through the lens of the central characters. I know that "show, don't tell" is the hallmark of good storytelling, but there was a disconnect in my opinion in the driving narrative and the grandiose asides of the state of humanity that Underworld posited.

Overall, a decent book, curious to read more DeLillo, but definitely could have been punchier which would have led to more enjoyment reading this opus.

Play It As It Lays

Joan Didion · ★★★★☆ · Aug 2026

Ennui is a series of rambling drives from San Pedro to Palm Desert, from Long Beach to Ventura and Oxnard, and back again, without discovering much about anything as the tires chew up the miles.

First Joan Didion novel, felt like this book was a breath of fresh air, with how sparsely and matter of factly it was written, every event is left up to the reader themselves to assign relative importance. The 60s were truly a wild time and Play It As It Lays to me, outlines how difficult it must have been to keep sane in the tumultuous, sun-kissed yet drug-tinged hills of Hollywood, especially as a woman. I would say that's showbiz baby, but i know nothing about showbiz.

This might piss some people off but this is the book that Otessa Moshfegh thought she was writing when she wrote My Year of Rest and Relaxation.

The Minotaur Takes a Cigarette Break

Steven Sherrill · ★★★★☆ · Aug 2026

I thought I had read this book many years ago but suppose not.

An odd little book that I enjoyed, mostly by thinking about it in the couple hours after each reading session. I desperately tried to think about the broader point the book was trying to make - is the Minotaur an approximation of man? Of the faults and imperfections that separate mortals from immortals, represented by an individual that straddles both sides? Is it something deeper than that that I'm missing?

Ultimately the humdrum nature of the Minotaur's life, the monotony of this (cigarette break) of his life compared to the 5,000 years of life before it (which also seemed monotonous), seem to be the point - at least the point I'm taking away from it. Life is the point, living is the point. There is no grand finale, no blockbuster payoff - sometimes you just live a life and that's the beauty of it - whether you're carving prime rib or fixing a car or going through your nightly self-care routine. There's always something to look forward to, like that cigarette.

A Parade of Horribles (Dungeon Crawler Carl, #8)

Matt Dinniman · ★★★★☆ · Jun 2026

[ADVANCE COPY REVIEW]

"You will not break me."

There's a gradual shift in the tenor of DCC from LitRPG hijinks (skills! loot boxes! mobs!) to a more contemplative rumination of what it means to be human and maintain your sense of self and your sense of community in times of extreme duress and oppression. We start to see that shift after the Butcher's Masquerade yet I think A Parade of Horribles is when we concretely understand this isn't just a game anymore. The 10th level mechanism is simplistic which allows for the story development to flourish. This does not mean, however, that the mechanics of the game take a backseat - on the contrary, the butterfly effect of the Crawlers trying to break the dungeon and bend the rules of the game is what moves this story forward. I wouldn't be surprised if we move forward outside of the dungeon to wrap up this series. 5/5, will re-read advance copy, listen to the audiobook, and read the official copy in May.

The bloodiest book in the series with long protracted war scenes for the first time really opened up the world beyond Darrow’s battles. Positives of the book include a flushing out of the alternative points of view and narrators in introduced in the last book as well as tension between doing what is right and doing what is good for yourself, your allies, and humanity. The Serviceberry: Abundance and Reciprocity in the Natural World

Robin Wall Kimmerer · ★★★★☆ · Jan 2026

This book was a very timely read as we enter 2026. With all of the capitalist- motivated actions of an increasing amount of governments, including our own, the author introduces and explores the idea of alternative economies specifically the gift economy. Group over self, reciprocity over selfishness, and dismantling preconceived notions of what it means to be both successful and happy as an individual and as part of a functioning society are all great reminders to keep others in your heart. Finished listening to this book as I head to my fun little capitalist job. Would recommend and at a two hour audiobook. It’s a great listen.

Read what makes you happy, and this makes me happy - the political and socioeconomic commentary is not heavy handed but there's just enough to make you pause and think about where you've seen this in real life before - i have played a fair amount of RPGs and watched a fair amount of reality tv and I love the world-building. However, it does leave room for my dumb ass t0 continue being surprised by a plot device placed 2.5 novels ago in a throwaway AI comment.

I think what also calls to me especially with the 7th installment is the shift in tone, the somber tone finally dominating rather than the wacky comic relief. [spoilers removed]
